IBS-C vs IBS-D: why the distinction changes your treatment

Updated IBS subtype treatment options, AGA & ACG Guidelines.

IBS & FODMAPsWhen to see a GI docConstipationDiarrhea

Educational content only. Not medical advice. Not a substitute for your physician's judgment. If you have new or worsening symptoms, talk to your own doctor.

If you have IBS and your doctor has prescribed you a medication without asking whether you're IBS-C or IBS-D, that prescription is probably wrong.

Not wrong-as-in-dangerous. Wrong-as-in-it's-the-wrong-drug-for-your-subtype. The 2022 AGA clinical practice guidelines on IBS — separate guidelines for IBS-C (Chang et al., Gastroenterology 2022, PMID 35738724) and IBS-D (Lembo et al., Gastroenterology 2022, PMID 35738725) — recommend different first-line medications for each subtype. Not different doses. Different drugs.

The subtype distinction matters more than anything else in this 12-week series. This article walks through:

  1. What the IBS subtypes actually are (Rome IV)
  2. How to figure out which subtype you have (home classification)
  3. The subtype-specific treatment ladders per AGA 2022
  4. What to do if you're IBS-M (mixed — the trickiest)
  5. The one question to ask your doctor at your next visit

1. What the IBS subtypes actually are (Rome IV criteria)

IBS is not one disease. It's a collection of related symptoms, classified by the predominant bowel habit pattern. The Rome IV criteria — the formal diagnostic standard — define four subtypes based on stool form (not symptoms, not pain, not frequency):

Subtype Rome IV definition (stool form on days with abnormal stools)
IBS-C (constipation-predominant) >25% hard stools (Bristol 1–2) AND <25% loose stools (Bristol 6–7)
IBS-D (diarrhea-predominant) >25% loose stools (Bristol 6–7) AND <25% hard stools (Bristol 1–2)
IBS-M (mixed) >25% hard stools AND >25% loose stools
IBS-U (unclassified) Insufficient abnormality to meet C, D, or M criteria

Two important things this table doesn't say:

  • Pain is not the classifier. Pain is required for the IBS diagnosis but doesn't determine the subtype. You can have severe pain with IBS-C or IBS-D; the subtype is determined by stool form alone.
  • Subtype is not permanent. Roughly 30–40% of IBS patients change subtype over 2–5 years. IBS-C can become IBS-M, IBS-D can become IBS-C (especially as women approach menopause), and IBS-M is genuinely unstable. If you were told you have IBS-C three years ago and your symptoms now look like IBS-D, the subtype should be re-evaluated.

The AGA 2022 guidelines for IBS-C and IBS-D were built around the Rome IV subtype definitions. Treating IBS-C with an IBS-D drug — or vice versa — is not a benign error; it can make symptoms worse.

2. How to figure out which subtype you have

You can do this at home with two weeks of data.

The Bristol Stool Form Scale classifies stool into seven types:

  • Type 1: Separate hard lumps (hard to pass)
  • Type 2: Lumpy, sausage-shaped
  • Type 3: Cracked, sausage-shaped
  • Type 4: Smooth, soft sausage or snake (ideal)
  • Type 5: Soft blobs with clear edges
  • Type 6: Mushy, fluffy pieces
  • Type 7: Entirely liquid

The home classification exercise:

  1. For 14 days, score every bowel movement using the Bristol scale. (Apps help — search "Bristol Stool Scale" in your phone's app store; free options exist.)
  2. Discard days with Bristol 3–4 (normal stools) — they don't count for subtype classification.
  3. Of the days with abnormal stools (Bristol 1–2 or 6–7): what percentage were 1–2 (hard) vs 6–7 (loose)?

The classification:

  • If >25% of abnormal days were Bristol 1–2 AND <25% were Bristol 6–7 → IBS-C
  • If >25% of abnormal days were Bristol 6–7 AND <25% were Bristol 1–2 → IBS-D
  • If both >25% → IBS-M
  • If neither → IBS-U (reassess)

Bring this 14-day diary to your next appointment. It's the single most useful data point you can give a gastroenterologist for subtype classification. Most patients (and many PCPs) classify by self-report ("I usually have diarrhea") which is unreliable. The 14-day diary is the evidence.

3. The subtype-specific treatment ladders per AGA 2022

IBS-D — first-line, per AGA 2022 (Lembo et al., PMID 35738725)

The AGA 2022 IBS-D guideline made 8 conditional recommendations FOR medications (no Strong recommendations in this guideline):

First-line (in order of evidence quality):

  • Rifaximin 550 mg three times daily for 14 days (repeatable) — Conditional FOR (moderate certainty). Same as ACG 2021 (Strong FOR, moderate evidence); the two societies agree.
  • Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) such as amitriptyline 10–25 mg at bedtime — Conditional FOR (low certainty). Particularly useful when pain is a major component.

Second-line / severe-disease:

  • Alosetron (5-HT3 antagonist) for women with severe IBS-D who have failed conventional therapy — Conditional FOR (moderate certainty). REMS-restricted; specialist prescription.
  • Eluxadoline (mixed opioid agonist/antagonist) — Conditional FOR (moderate certainty). Avoid in patients without a gallbladder or with pancreatitis/SOD history.

Adjunct / rescue:

  • Loperamide (Imodium) for predictable episodes (long car ride, important meeting) — Conditional FOR (very low certainty). Not a global IBS-D therapy; works on transit but not on pain or bloating.

What AGA 2022 IBS-D says NOT to use:

  • SSRIs — Conditional AGAINST (low certainty). TCAs are preferred over SSRIs for IBS.

IBS-C — first-line, per AGA 2022 (Chang et al., PMID 35738724)

The AGA 2022 IBS-C guideline made 9 recommendations (one Strong, eight Conditional):

There is two different mechanisms: osmotic laxatives (usually available over the counter) which work by drawing and keeping water in the lumen of the intestines and lead to softer stools that are easier to pass and secretagogues that lead to softer stools by causing the lining of the intestine to secret more electolytes into the lumen of the intestines which in turn draw fluid with them and soften the stool. Oftentimes these secretagogues have additional properties that can help alleviate abdominal pain beyond preventing hard stools.

First-line (Strong recommendation):

  • Linaclotide 290 µg daily — Strong FOR, high certainty. This is a secretagogue. It is the only Strong recommendation in either AGA 2022 guideline. If you have IBS-C and aren't responding to lifestyle/dietary measures, linaclotide is the prescription with the strongest evidence base.

First-line alternatives (Conditional FOR, moderate certainty):

  • Plecanatide 3 mg daily — similar mechanism to linaclotide.
  • Tenapanor 50 mg twice daily — newer agent; different mechanism (intestinal sodium-hydrogen exchanger 3 inhibition).
  • Lubiprostone 8 µg twice daily — chloride channel activator.
  • Tegaserod (5-HT4 agonist) — Conditional FOR (moderate certainty). Restricted to women under 65 with ≤1 cardiovascular risk factor who have not adequately responded to secretagogues. FDA cardiovascular-safety restriction; specialist prescription only.

Second-line / rescue (Conditional FOR, low certainty):

  • PEG laxatives (MiraLAX) — Conditional FOR (low certainty). This is an osmotic laxative. Reasonable as a rescue agent or while waiting for secretagogues to work, not first-line per AGA 2022 either.
  • Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs) — Conditional FOR (low certainty).

What AGA 2022 IBS-C says NOT to use:

  • SSRIs — Conditional AGAINST (low certainty). Same as IBS-D.

The two subtypes compared — at a glance

Step IBS-D IBS-C
Diet/fiber first Soluble fiber (Strong FOR ACG) Soluble fiber (Strong FOR ACG)
Rx first-line Rifaximin (AGA Conditional FOR / ACG Strong FOR) Linaclotide (AGA Strong FOR)
Rx if pain prominent Low-dose TCA (AGA Conditional FOR) Low-dose TCA (AGA Conditional FOR)
Rx rescue Loperamide for episodes PEG for occasional use
Severe disease Alosetron (women) / eluxadoline Tegaserod (women <65, no CV risk)
AVOID SSRIs SSRIs

The key contrast: IBS-D's first-line prescription is an antibiotic (rifaximin); IBS-C's first-line prescription is a guanylate cyclase activator (linaclotide). These are completely different drug classes, working through completely different mechanisms. A doctor who treats both subtypes with "low-dose antidepressants" is using one drug for two different diseases. It works for the pain component of both, but it doesn't address the underlying motility problem in either.

4. What to do if you're IBS-M (mixed)

IBS-M is the most common subtype but also the most clinically frustrating — and the AGA 2022 guidelines don't specifically address it. Real-world clinical approach:

  1. Treat the dominant pattern over the most recent 2–4 weeks. If your last month was 70% diarrhea days and 30% constipation days, treat as IBS-D for that month. Reassess monthly.
  2. Avoid narrow-spectrum agents. Drugs like linaclotide (strong pro-secretory) can swing you hard into diarrhea territory if you're IBS-M and a "constipation week" becomes a "diarrhea week." If you have IBS-M and need IBS-C treatment, tenapanor or low-dose lubiprostone may be more forgiving than full-dose linaclotide.
  3. Mind the overlap drugs. TCAs are the most "subtype-neutral" of the prescription options — they treat pain and global symptoms without strongly driving motility in either direction. If you're IBS-M with significant pain, TCAs may be the most subtype-appropriate first-line prescription.
  4. Dietary management is more important than pharmacological for IBS-M. The low-FODMAP restriction with structured reintroduction is the single highest-leverage intervention (per AGA 2022 Diet CPU, Chey et al., PMID 35337654), because it identifies your specific food triggers rather than broadly modulating motility.

5. The one question to ask your doctor at your next visit

"Based on my 14-day Bristol Stool diary, what's my IBS subtype, and what subtype-specific treatment are you recommending?"

If your doctor doesn't ask to see the diary (or doesn't ask about your stool form on the days you have abnormal stools), the subtype classification is happening by guess — and the prescription is probably wrong.

If your doctor has prescribed you a medication and you don't know whether it's subtype-appropriate, ask:

  • "Is this for IBS-C, IBS-D, or both?"
  • "Which AGA or ACG recommendation does this correspond to?"

A gastroenterologist who knows their guidelines will answer both questions in under 30 seconds.

6. What to do this week

  1. Start a 14-day Bristol Stool diary (apps: search "Bristol Stool Scale"; paper works fine too).
  2. Classify yourself using the rules in §2 of this article.
  3. Read Week 3 (publishes 2026-09-15): "IBS treatment in 2026: what actually works" — the full pharmacological ladder, anchored to the AGA 2022 guidelines.
  4. If you've been on an IBS medication and aren't sure it's subtype-appropriate, ask your doctor the §5 question above. If you've been on the same med for >6 months with no clear improvement, that's worth flagging too.
