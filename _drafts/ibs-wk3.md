# IBS treatment in 2026: what actually works

If you've been prescribed an IBS medication, there's a real chance your doctor is reaching for something the 2022 AGA (American Gastroenterological Association) clinical practice guidelines would rank as "Conditional FOR" — or worse, "Conditional AGAINST."

This article walks you through every common IBS treatment, ranked by the evidence tier the AGA 2022 guidelines assigned to it. The point isn't to make you self-prescribe. The point is to walk into your next appointment a little bit smarter than before and to have a meaningful and informed discussion with your healthcare provider and to help you reach a successful treatment faster. Knowing what the evidence actually says — and what your prescription's tier means for whether you should expect it to work - will empower you to make an informed decision in your treatment plan.

We will cover, in order:
1. How IBS (Irritable Bowel Syndrome) treatments get ranked by evidence (and why most of them unfortunately rank low)
2. The IBS-D (D for diarrhea) treatment ladder per AGA 2022 (Lembo et al.)
3. The IBS-C (C for constipation) treatment ladder per AGA 2022 (Chang et al.)
4. The diet + behavioral interventions (and where they actually rank)
5. The placebo effect in IBS — the single most important thing to know about IBS treatment trials
6. The SSRIs-AGAINST recommendation that most PCPs don't know about
7. The medications your doctor might offer that the guidelines actively recommend against
8. What to ask your doctor at your next visit

## 1. How IBS treatments get ranked by evidence (and why most of them rank low)

The AGA 2022 clinical practice guidelines on IBS-D (Lembo et al.) and IBS-C (Chang et al.) used the **GRADE methodology** to grade every treatment. GRADE has three tiers the panel assigns:

- **Strong FOR** — high-quality evidence; the panel is confident this works. *Only one IBS treatment earned this tier in either AGA 2022 guideline: linaclotide for IBS-C.*
- **Conditional FOR** — the evidence is positive but the panel has less confidence; the recommendation is "yes, but with caveats." *Most IBS medications are here.*
- **Conditional AGAINST** — the panel actively recommends against using this. *SSRIs for IBS-D and IBS-C are in this tier.*

The uncomfortable truth is that **most IBS medications are Conditional FOR, not Strong FOR.** The only drug with Strong FOR is linaclotide for IBS-C, and even that one comes with a 14-30% discontinuation rate due to diarrhea.

This matters because:
- A doctor prescribing you a Conditional FOR treatment is doing something that's *probably helpful but not guaranteed.* The patient should know that.
- A doctor prescribing you a Conditional AGAINST treatment is doing something that the AGA panel actively recommends against. I want you to know that especially.

The standard you should hold your doctor to: **they should know which tier your prescription is in, and they should be able to explain why they chose that tier over the alternatives.**

## 2. The IBS-D treatment ladder per AGA 2022 (Lembo et al., PMID 35738725)

The AGA 2022 IBS-D guideline made **8 conditional recommendations**. **No Strong recommendations** — meaning no IBS-D drug has the same evidence confidence as linaclotide has for IBS with constipation.

**First-line (Conditional FOR, moderate certainty):**

- **Rifaximin** 550 mg three times daily for 14 days (repeatable) — the AGA's preferred first-line agent. The ACG 2021 guideline (Lacy et al., PMID 33315591) gives rifaximin a Strong FOR for the same indication, so the two major societies agree this is the best-tested option. Mechanism: non-absorbable antibiotic - so this antibiotic cannot give you a UTI or affect your body in any other way - that can alter your gut flora. Effect size: ~10% better than placebo over 10 weeks and shown to be a significant effect in some studies.

**Second-line:**

- **Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)** such as amitriptyline 10–25 mg at bedtime — Conditional FOR, low certainty. Particularly useful when pain dominates the picture. Effect size: ~15% better than placebo at 12 weeks. If amitriptyline causes too much drowsiness, you can try nortriptyline as an alternative.
- **Alosetron** 0.5–1 mg twice daily for women with severe IBS-D who have failed conventional therapy — Conditional FOR, moderate certainty. REMS-restricted due to ischemic colitis risk. Specialist prescription.
- **Eluxadoline** 100 mg twice daily — Conditional FOR, moderate certainty. Avoid in patients without a gallbladder or with pancreatitis or sphincter of Oddi dysfunction history. Specialist prescription.

**Rescue:**

- **Loperamide** (Imodium) 2–4 mg as needed for predictable episodes — Conditional FOR, very low certainty. Works on transit (slows it) but doesn't address pain or bloating. Useful for "I have a long car ride tomorrow" situations, not as a daily therapy.

**What AGA 2022 actively recommends AGAINST for IBS-D:**

- **SSRIs** (e.g., sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine for IBS-D) — Conditional AGAINST, low certainty. This is significant because many PCPs reach for SSRIs first when a patient has chronic abdominal symptoms with no clear organic cause. The AGA panel looked at the data and concluded SSRIs don't help IBS-D. (TCAs work; SSRIs don't. The class effect doesn't transfer.)

## 3. The IBS-C treatment ladder per AGA 2022 (Chang et al., PMID 35738724)

The AGA 2022 IBS-C guideline made **9 recommendations** with a different evidence distribution than IBS-D: **1 Strong FOR** + **8 Conditional FORs / AGAINSTs**.

**First-line (Strong FOR, high certainty — the only Strong FOR in either AGA 2022 guideline):**

- **Linaclotide** 290 µg daily — *the single highest-evidence IBS medication in either AGA 2022 guideline.* Effect size: ~15-20% better than placebo at 12 weeks. Main side effect: diarrhea (causes discontinuation in 14-30% of patients, especially at first; the dose can be reduced to 145 µg if needed). In practice, however, most providers start patients on the medium dose of 145 µg daily, to be able to pivot to the lowest dose possible which is 72 µg daily, in case of diarrhea. If the medium dose is not effective or just partially effective, you can double the dose to achieve the recommended 290 µg daily.

**First-line alternatives (Conditional FOR, moderate certainty):**

- **Plecanatide** 3 mg daily — same drug class as linaclotide (guanylate cyclase-C agonist). Similar efficacy, slightly different side effect profile.
- **Tenapanor** 50 mg twice daily — newer agent; different mechanism (intestinal sodium-hydrogen exchanger 3 inhibitor). Useful when linaclotide/plecanatide cause too much diarrhea.
- **Lubiprostone** 8 µg twice daily — chloride channel activator. Older drug; efficacy is moderate.
- **Tegaserod** 6 mg twice daily — 5-HT4 agonist. Restricted to women under 65 with ≤1 cardiovascular risk factor who have not adequately responded to secretagogues. CV-safety restriction per FDA.

**Rescue / second-line (Conditional FOR, low certainty):**

- **PEG laxatives (MiraLAX)** — Conditional FOR. Reasonable as a rescue agent or while waiting for secretagogues to work. *Note: the ACG 2021 guideline is Conditional Against for global IBS-C symptoms because PEG softens stool but doesn't reliably address pain or bloating. In some cases bloating and bloating associated abdominal cramping can be intensified.* Net read: PEG is OK for occasional use but not as primary therapy.

**What AGA 2022 actively recommends AGAINST for IBS-C:**

- **SSRIs** — same Conditional AGAINST as for IBS-D.
- **Bile acid sequestrants** (e.g., cholestyramine) — Conditional AGAINST per ACG 2021 (Rec #19) for global IBS-D symptoms. Useful only if you have a confirmed bile acid malabsorption, which is uncommon.

## 4. Diet + behavioral interventions (where they actually rank)

Not all of IBS treatment is pharmacological. Three non-drug categories deserve specific framing.

**Diet:**

- **Low-FODMAP diet (4–6 week restriction + structured reintroduction)** — the AGA 2022 Diet CPU (Chey et al., PMID 35337654) endorses this as the most evidence-based dietary intervention. Effect size: ~50-70% of patients report meaningful symptom improvement. Important: do not stay on indefinite strict elimination (changes microbiome; risks nutritional gaps). Reintroduction phase is where the learning happens. If this feels too extreme an intervention, I also discuss with my patients, whether they have already identified certain foods that can trigger symptoms especially and to selectively cut those out first, before trying a strict low FODMAP diet which is not necessarily known to be a healthy nutritious diet.
- **Soluble fiber (psyllium)** — Strong FOR per ACG 2021 (Rec #11). Start at 5 g/day, titrate to 20 g/day. Cheap, low-risk, very decent evidence. It is, however, important to increase your dose slowly and once you do notice bloating, go back to the lower dose prior. Caveat: Drink plenty of fluids alongside, that is the only way the increase in fiber can actually work and be beneficial, otherwise it can worsen symptoms.
- **Insoluble fiber (wheat bran)** — NOT recommended. Often makes bloating worse despite the reflexive "just eat more fiber" advice.

**Behavioral therapies (ACG Rec #24; AGA Pain CPU 2021):**

- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** — Conditional FOR per ACG 2021. Effect size: comparable to medications in some head-to-head studies. This is one of the most publicly underrated interventions that are at your disposal. There are FDA-approved apps, and plenty of free resources online as well. Take advantage!!!
- **Gut-directed hypnotherapy** — Conditional FOR per ACG 2021. Strongest evidence in IBS specifically (not just general anxiety). Unfortunately being able to find a niche practitioner is very hard, even in urban areas, and is the bottleneck.
- **Mindfulness-based therapy** — Conditional FOR. Less IBS-specific evidence but general stress-reduction benefits DO apply.

**Probiotics:**

- **AC specifically against** as a class (Conditional AGAINST per ACG 2021 Rec #14). Some specific strains have positive trials, but the class effect is not supported. If your doctor says "try a probiotic" without specifying strain + dose + trial duration, that's not evidence-based care.

## 5. The placebo effect in IBS — the single most important thing to know about IBS treatment trials

A 2020 meta-analysis of IBS randomized controlled trials (PMID 31929200, Patel et al., Aliment Pharmacol Ther) found that **the average placebo response rate in IBS trials is 30–40%**. This is **higher than placebo response rates in most other chronic conditions** (depression trials average ~35%; migraine trials ~25%; hypertension trials ~20%).

What this means practically:

- When you try an IBS treatment and "it kind of works," there's a 30-40% chance the improvement you noticed is the placebo effect, not the treatment.
- This is why IBS trials need large sample sizes and active comparators — the placebo effect is so strong that small studies frequently show "treatments" working when they're really measuring the placebo response.
- **When your doctor switches you from one IBS drug to another and the new one "works better," there's a real chance that's a fresh placebo response, not the new drug being better.**

The clinical takeaway: if a treatment works for 3 months and then stops working, it might be that the placebo response has worn off — not that the disease has progressed. This is normal; cycling treatments is sometimes the right move. **This also supports the idea of the gut-brain axis in IBS and points towards the strong effect your psyche and state of mind can have on your symptoms. I cannot stress enough the importance of cognitive behavioral therapy and stress reducing measures in this context.**

## 6. The SSRIs-AGAINST recommendation that most PCPs don't know about

This is the recommendation I most often have to explain to patients. SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors — sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine, escitalopram, etc.) are the most-prescribed class of psychiatric medications in the US. Many PCPs reach for them first when a patient has chronic abdominal symptoms with no clear organic cause.

**The AGA 2022 guideline actively recommends AGAINST SSRIs for both IBS-D and IBS-C.** Conditional AGAINST, low certainty.

The reason matters: TCAs (tricyclic antidepressants — amitriptyline, nortriptyline, desipramine) are **different** from SSRIs, even though both are "antidepressants." TCAs have evidence for IBS (ACG Strong FOR; AGA Conditional FOR). SSRIs have evidence AGAINST for IBS (AGA Conditional AGAINST).

If your doctor is prescribing you sertraline "for IBS," that's an evidence-against move per AGA 2022. If they're prescribing low-dose amitriptyline "for IBS pain," that's an evidence-for move. Both are "antidepressants"; the IBS evidence is opposite.

This is also the most common thing I see PCPs get wrong about IBS prescribing.

## 7. Medications your doctor might offer that the guidelines actively recommend against

Four classes of medications are actively NOT recommended for IBS by either the ACG 2021 or AGA 2022 guidelines, but they still get prescribed:

- **SSRIs for IBS-D or IBS-C** — AGA 2022 Conditional AGAINST. Use TCAs instead.
- **Bile acid sequestrants for global IBS-D symptoms** — ACG 2021 Rec #19 Conditional AGAINST. Useful only with confirmed bile acid malabsorption which is a rare diagnosis.
- **Antispasmodics available in the US** (dicyclomine, hyoscyamine, hyoscine) — ACG 2021 Rec #12 Conditional AGAINST. Weak modern evidence; peppermint oil is a better alternative.
- **Probiotics as a class** — ACG 2021 Rec #14 Conditional AGAINST. Some specific strains have positive trials; the class effect is not supported.

If your doctor is prescribing any of these "for IBS," ask which evidence tier the prescription is in. They may have a good reason (e.g., they're prescribing an SSRI for comorbid depression, not for IBS — that's appropriate). Or they may not know the AGA 2022 recommendation. Worth the 30-second conversation.

## 8. What to ask your doctor at your next visit

Three questions, in order:

1. **"Based on my 14-day Bristol Stool diary, what's my IBS subtype, and what subtype-specific treatment are you recommending?"** (From Week 2; this question comes first because subtype determines which ladder applies.)
2. **"What evidence tier is my prescription in — Strong FOR, Conditional FOR, or Conditional AGAINST?"** (Most prescriptions will be Conditional FOR. That's OK to know.)
3. **"What should I do if this prescription doesn't work, or stops working after 3 months?"** (The answer should include a second-line option from the same subtype ladder. If the doctor doesn't have one, that's a sign they need to the current AGA 2022 guidelines.)

If your doctor can answer all three questions confidently and in under 30 seconds each, you're seeing someone who knows the current IBS evidence base. If they can't, that's a useful data point for whether you want a second opinion or a referral to a gastroenterologist who subscribes to AGA guidelines.
